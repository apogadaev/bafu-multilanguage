# Community Review with Confidence Scoring for Annual Dataset Translation: A Practical Report

## TL;DR
- **Yes, the pattern makes sense and is well-precedented.** LLM translation → automated confidence/quality-estimation (QE) scoring → community/volunteer review → per-row/per-language publish gate is essentially how Wikipedia Content Translation, Mozilla Common Voice, TED Translators, Weblate, Crowdin, Cohere's Aya, and Masakhane already operate. Your instinct to make it the *primary* model — with agency post-editing (ISO 18587), in-house TMS review, and scripted pipelines as fallbacks — is defensible for an annual, open-data batch.
- **The confidence score is the load-bearing part and the riskiest.** Reference-free QE (COMETKiwi/xCOMET) is good at ranking overall quality but is a known blind spot for fluent hallucinations, numbers/dates, and glossary/placeholder errors — exactly the errors that matter in structured data. Treat QE as a *triage router*, calibrate its thresholds on a human-labeled sample, and always pair it with deterministic rule checks and random spot-checks of "high-confidence" rows.
- **Design the gate around consensus + provenance, not raw MT.** Use two-tier roles (contributor/reviewer + language coordinator), 2-vote agreement à la Common Voice, gold/control items to measure reviewer accuracy, structured MQM-style feedback, per-row and per-language publish rules, and a data model that stores model/prompt version, QE score, votes, reviewer IDs, and status so you can diff and reuse next year. Mind GDPR (you're in the EU) and pick a contributor license (CC0/CC BY-SA) up front.

## Key Findings

**1. The workflow is an established pattern, not a novel gamble.** Every major community-localization system combines machine pre-translation, some confidence/agreement signal, human review, and a publish gate:
- **Wikipedia Content Translation** blocks publication when the share of *unmodified* machine translation exceeds a per-wiki threshold. Per MediaWiki's help documentation, "Publication is blocked if 95% or more of the whole document consists of unmodified, machine translated content" — a global limit that Wikimedia lowered from the older 99% to 95% in March 2023 (Phabricator T330482). Individual communities negotiate stricter limits: Indonesian Wikipedia set 70%, and its notice tells editors "You can only publish if you can rephrase or modify the machine translation content by 30%"; Telugu and Chinese communities requested comparable 60–70% limits. This is a direct real-world "publish gate keyed to a machine-confidence proxy."
- **Mozilla Common Voice** validates by simple 2-vote consensus. Per Ardila et al. (2020), "A maximum of three contributors will listen to any audio clip. If an ⟨audio,transcript⟩ pair first receives two up-votes, then the clip is marked as valid. If instead the clip first receives two down-votes, then it is marked as invalid." This is the canonical lightweight consensus gate.
- **TED Translators (OTP)** uses a strict translate → review → approve pipeline with tiered roles and earned rights. The TED Translators Wiki states, "we require that only volunteers with at least 90 minutes of transcribed talks begin reviewing transcripts, and only volunteers with at least 90 minutes of translated talks begin reviewing translations," with final approval reserved for a Language Coordinator, 30-day task windows, and mentoring/feedback — an apprenticeship model that doubles as quality control.
- **Cohere Aya** built a 204,114-pair instruction dataset across 65 languages via an open-science volunteer community on a purpose-built annotation platform (Singh et al., 2024), with collaborators from 119 countries, released under Apache 2.0, using a find-fix-verify workflow with peer review. It also surfaced the characteristic failure: a long tail of one-or-two-contribution annotators and uneven per-language coverage.
- **Masakhane** demonstrated participatory, volunteer MT for African languages: Nekoto et al. (2020) report a community of 144 participants from 17 African countries producing MT benchmarks for over 30 languages (a later count cites 46 benchmarks for 39 languages), explicitly showing the approach "enables participants without formal training to make a unique scientific contribution" — while also showing that coverage and quality vary widely across languages.
- **Weblate and Crowdin** implement exactly the roles/votes/gate machinery out of the box (suggest → vote → reviewer-approve → commit-only-approved), and both are free for open-source projects.

**2. Confidence scoring works as a router but must be calibrated and backstopped.** Reference-free QE models (Unbabel COMETKiwi, xCOMET) output a 0–1 quality score from source+translation with no reference needed. They correlate well at the system level but only moderately at the segment level, and they systematically miss the errors most damaging to a dataset: fluent hallucinations, wrong numbers/dates, and format/placeholder breaks. The fix is a layered signal (QE + rule checks + optional LLM-as-judge) plus threshold calibration on a few hundred human-labeled segments and mandatory random sampling of high-confidence rows.

**3. The publish decision should be a consensus + gate, governed by provenance.** Combine per-row rules (enough votes, agreement, no unresolved critical flags) with per-language gates (minimum reviewer coverage, MQM/ISO 5060 sample score on a random draw). Everything hinges on a data model that records the provenance needed to diff next year and reuse approved rows.

## Details

### Verdict: does the proposed model make sense?
**Yes — with three conditions.** (a) Confidence scoring is used only to *route and prioritize*, never to auto-publish unseen; (b) a deterministic rule layer guards numbers, dates, placeholders, and glossary terms that QE ignores; (c) the review layer produces *consensus* with measured reviewer reliability, not a single anchored opinion. Under those conditions the model is squarely in line with how Wikipedia, Mozilla, TED, Cohere, and the open-source TMS ecosystem already work. It is *not* appropriate as the sole mechanism for legal, medical, or safety-critical fields (see Caveats).

### Reference architecture for the annual cycle
1. **Ingest & diff.** Load the new English batch. For every record, compute a stable key from the row's persistent ID plus a hash of the source field(s). Diff against last year: unchanged source + previously approved translation ⇒ carry the approval forward (no re-review); changed or new source ⇒ enqueue for translation.
2. **Translate.** Run the LLM per target language with a versioned prompt, injecting glossary terms, do-not-translate tokens, and placeholder rules into the prompt. Store model name, prompt version, and timestamp.
3. **Score confidence (multi-signal).**
   - *QE model*: COMETKiwi (fast, 0–1) and/or xCOMET (adds error-span detection) per segment/field.
   - *Rule checks* (deterministic, pass/fail): placeholder/tag parity, number/date/unit consistency, glossary adherence, length sanity, script/locale correctness (BCP 47/CLDR).
   - *Optional LLM-as-judge* (GEMBA-MQM style) for borderline or high-value rows.
   - *Optional agreement*: sample the LLM 2–3× or use a second model; divergence = lower confidence. Back-translation similarity as a cheap secondary check.
4. **Bucket & route.** High confidence (QE above the calibrated pass line AND all rule checks pass) → light path (spot-check only). Medium → full community review. Low (below pass line OR any critical rule failure) → priority review or re-translation, or paid review for languages with no volunteers.
5. **Community review.** Publish rows into the review UI in context. Contributors approve/edit/flag with MQM-style category+severity. Collect ≥2 independent judgments; reviewers/coordinators adjudicate disagreements.
6. **Aggregate & gate.** Apply per-row and per-language publish rules (below). Rows that fail the gate fall back to English with a "machine translated / under review" label.
7. **Publish & feed back.** Publish approved rows per language. Push accepted edits into translation memory (TMX) and glossary, and fold recurring corrections into next year's prompt. Store all provenance.

### Confidence scoring: concrete thresholds and calibration
- **What the score is.** COMETKiwi (`Unbabel/wmt22-cometkiwi-da`, built on InfoXLM, trained on WMT DA + MLQE-PE) returns a scalar 0–1; xCOMET adds error-span detection and severity and has the highest MQM correlation of the family. Scores are natively 0–1 but many papers report ×100 — confirm the scale before setting a cutoff.
- **Reliability.** These metrics are strong at the *system* level (Pearson often 0.9+) but only *moderate* at the *segment* level. In the WMT22 QE shared task, COMETKiwi's MQM segment-level Spearman correlations were en-de 0.553 and en-ru 0.569 — but only 0.268 for the distant zh-en pair (Rei et al., 2022). So per-row scores are noisy, and noisier still for typologically distant languages: use buckets, not fine-grained trust.
- **The dangerous failure mode.** QE metrics are relatively insensitive to exactly the errors that ruin a dataset: numbers/dates, named entities, and format. Amrhein & Sennrich (2022) found COMET's "low sensitivity … to perturbations of numbers and named entities"; Guerreiro et al. (2022) showed "COMET and COMET-QE scores do not provide separation between hallucinations and less severe errors." Intento's requirements-based study of 84,000 translations found that "46% [of 9,217 requirement violations] are above 0.90 on the COMET scale of 0 to 1." This is why a rule layer and random sampling of high-confidence rows are non-negotiable. xCOMET-XXL is materially better — assigning "over 90% of … fully detached hallucinations a score under 10" on a 0–100 scale — but is still imperfect, and consistent cross-study weakness remains on **numbers/dates and locale formatting**.
- **Starting thresholds (calibrate before trusting).** Vendor guidance buckets a 0–1 QE roughly as: TAUS's interpretation is "Above 0.9: good translations; 0.88–0.9: a gray area; Below 0.88: usually … at least minor errors; Below 0.8: serious errors." Custom.MT recommends setting "high QE thresholds (95+) for critical content and flexible scores (75–80) for low-risk internal docs." A published cApStAn survey-data case set a calibrated COMET cutoff of 0.81 (post-editing everything at or below it), then spot-checked a sample above 0.81 where "over 95% of the segments passed the quality check." Treat these as starting points: set the pass line *higher* (≈0.90–0.95) for higher-stakes fields, lower (≈0.75–0.80) for low-risk fields.
- **How to calibrate.** Draw a representative sample per language pair, hand-label each with a human quality judgment (MQM annotation or a 0–5 score), and correlate QE against the human labels (Pearson/Spearman). TAUS advises a gold set of "at least 500 real-world translation segments"; Custom.MT's workflow uses "300 [as] a representative sample" of good sentences plus a sample of error sentences, then picks "a threshold where the model may be trusted, for example, 90," measuring the pass rate of good vs. error sentences at that line. Then manually review the disagreements. Practical consensus: **~300–500 mixed good/bad segments** per language pair.
- **Layering catches what QE misses.** Requirements-based/rule LQA catches locale, glossary, and placeholder violations that QE rates highly (Intento found a violation scored *higher* than a compliant translation "36 to 47% of the time"). A QE→APE→QE two-pass (auto-post-edit only the low-scoring segments, then re-score) reduces human load. LLM-as-judge (GEMBA-MQM) reached 96.5% system-level pairwise accuracy on the WMT23 blind set but is slower/costlier and weaker at segment level; reserve it for borderline/high-value rows. Question-answer-based checks (e.g., AskQE) outperform xCOMET-QE on catching critical biomedical errors (decision accuracy ~76% vs. ~64%).

### Volunteer review design
- **Roles/tiers.** Mirror TED/Weblate/Pontoon: (1) contributor (suggest/edit), (2) reviewer/approver (trusted, can approve), (3) language coordinator (owns a language, breaks ties, onboards). New users start in suggestion-only mode; approval rights are earned (TED's ≥90-minutes-of-prior-work rule).
- **Consensus rules.** Adopt Common Voice's 2-vote rule as the baseline: a row needs ≥2 independent approvals with no unresolved critical flag; 2 rejections send it back. For contentious rows, require a coordinator decision. Weighted voting (trusted users count more) as used by Crowdin/Facebook helps once you have a reputation signal.
- **Measuring reviewer accuracy.** Seed gold/control items (rows with known-correct or known-bad translations) into review queues to measure each reviewer's accuracy and down-weight or retrain unreliable reviewers. Track inter-annotator agreement (Fleiss' κ / Krippendorff's α; production threshold typically κ > 0.6). Note majority voting can fail on genuinely hard items — expert/coordinator adjudication is the backstop.
- **Structured feedback.** Prefer approve / edit / flag-with-MQM-category-and-severity over free text — it produces measurable, aggregatable data (aligned with the MQM/ISO 5060 error typology: accuracy, fluency, terminology, style, locale/design, etc., each minor/major/critical) and feeds root-cause analysis for next year's prompt.
- **Anchoring bias.** Showing MT output pre-fills the answer and biases reviewers toward accepting it (documented in Argilla's own guidance on pre-annotations). Mitigate by asking for an explicit judgment before revealing the MT, hiding the QE score during review, and using blind gold items.
- **Vandalism/spam.** Use author-filtered bulk actions (as Pontoon does — filter and "delete all suggestions" from a spammer), profanity/blacklist filters, rate limits, earned trust tiers, and never auto-commit unreviewed suggestions.
- **Motivation/retention.** Recognition, leaderboards, badges, credits, and community (TED's Facebook language groups; Aya contributors motivated by bringing NLP to their language). Expect a long tail of one-off contributors (Aya) and plan for attrition.
- **Languages with few/no volunteers.** This is the hardest gap (Aya/Masakhane both hit it). Fallbacks: English fallback with a "machine translated" label, paid professional review (ISO 18587) for those languages only, or leave low-confidence rows unpublished.
- **Time windows.** Given an annual cycle, run a fixed review window (e.g., 30-day task expiry like TED) with reminders; unclaimed/expired tasks return to the pool.

### Aggregating feedback & the publish decision
- **Per-row rule (example).** Publish a row/language if: QE ≥ calibrated pass line OR ≥2 reviewer approvals; AND all deterministic rule checks pass; AND no unresolved critical/major MQM flag. Otherwise hold and fall back to English.
- **Per-language gate.** Only "open" a language for publication once it clears a random-sample MQM/ISO 5060 quality score (segment-based error scoring against source with severity weights) and has a minimum reviewer coverage. This lets you publish, say, French and Spanish while German waits.
- **Fallbacks.** English fallback; visible "machine-translated, pending review" label; targeted paid review for gaps; keep the row unpublished if it fails a critical check.
- **Feedback loop.** Route accepted edits into translation memory (TMX) and glossary; convert recurring error categories into explicit prompt rules and glossary entries for next year's LLM run.

### Annual-cycle specifics
- **Diffing.** Key each record by stable ID + source hash; only changed/new sources are re-translated and re-reviewed. Crowdin's raw-data model explicitly keys on stringId + source-text hash for exactly this reason ("multiple records can exist for the same stringId if the source hash … varies").
- **Reuse.** Carry forward approved translations whose source is unchanged; this shrinks each annual batch to the delta.
- **Re-engagement.** Re-invite prior volunteers before the window opens; recognize returning contributors; surface only the delta so the task feels finite.
- **Provenance (store per translation).** Source ID, source hash, target language (BCP 47), model name, prompt version, QE model+score, rule-check results, LLM-judge score (if any), reviewer IDs, votes/decisions, MQM flags, status (machine/needs-review/approved/rejected/published), timestamps.

### Standards, legal & ethical
- **Standards to align with.** ISO 18587:2017 (full/light post-editing of MT — your *paid fallback* path; note it applies only to MT-processed content and requires *professional* post-editors, so cite it for the fallback, not the community path); ISO 17100 (human translation services); ISO 5060:2024 (evaluation of translation output, MQM-harmonized error typology with minor/major/critical severities — use for your sample quality gate; it is guidance, not a pass/fail requirement); MQM (the error framework itself); BCP 47 (language tags) and CLDR (locale data) for correctness; XLIFF (interchange) and TMX (translation memory) for tooling portability.
- **Licensing of contributions.** Decide the contributor license up front and put it in the terms so volunteer edits can be published/redistributed. Wikimedia uses CC BY-SA for text (each user agrees to release contributions on edit) and CC0 for factual data (Wikidata); Aya used Apache 2.0. For an open dataset, CC0 (public-domain dedication, simplest reuse) or CC BY-SA (share-alike) are the standard choices.
- **GDPR (you're in the EU).** Storing reviewer identities/IDs is processing personal data. Under Art. 6 you need a lawful basis (consent or legitimate interest, typically via a volunteer agreement), a privacy notice, data minimization (store only what you need — pseudonymous IDs where possible), retention limits, and support for access/erasure requests, plus an audit trail of consent. Contributions may need to persist for attribution even if an account is deleted — address this explicitly in the terms.
- **When community review is NOT appropriate.** Legal, medical, and safety content: use professional post-editing (ISO 18587) or full human translation (ISO 17100), not volunteers.

### Tooling that supports this out of the box
- **Weblate** (free/OSS): suggestion + voting + reviewer-approve workflow; its commit policy can be set to "Only include approved translations" = your publish gate; built-in quality checks (placeholders, consistency); MT/glossary/TM integration. Strong default choice for self-hosting in the EU.
- **Crowdin** (free for OSS): community crowdsourcing with suggest/vote/proofread, MT pre-fill, glossary/TM, per-string approval, contributor reports (votes, approvals), and a raw-data model keyed on stringId+source hash.
- **Pontoon** (Mozilla, OSS): suggest → trusted-localizer approve; author-filtered bulk actions for spam; Git sync; already integrates LLM-assisted translation (rephrase/refine).
- **Transifex / Tolgee**: TMS with review workflows and MT; Tolgee is developer-friendly and open-source.
- **Label Studio / Argilla**: annotation-style review — best if you treat this as a *dataset labeling* task rather than software localization. Argilla lets you inject model "suggestions" (the LLM translation) as a starting point, define structured questions (approve/edit/MQM flag), control annotator overlap, and export — and warns explicitly about anchoring bias from pre-annotations.
- **Plugging in QE.** COMETKiwi/xCOMET run as Python models; compute scores in the pipeline before loading rows, store the score as metadata, and use it to route/prioritize which rows go into the review tool and in what order.

## Recommendations
1. **Adopt the community-review-with-confidence-scoring model as primary.** Stage it: Year 0 pilot on 2–3 high-resource languages with active volunteers; expand once the gate and calibration are proven.
2. **Build the multi-signal confidence layer first, and never auto-publish on QE alone.** Ship: COMETKiwi (and/or xCOMET) + a deterministic rule layer (placeholders, numbers/dates, glossary, locale) + random spot-checks of high-confidence rows. Add GEMBA-MQM only for borderline/high-value rows.
3. **Calibrate thresholds on ~300–500 human-labeled segments per language pair** before the first run; start conservative (pass line ~0.90 for important fields, ~0.80 for low-risk), and re-calibrate annually. **Benchmark that changes the plan:** if spot-checks of "high-confidence" rows reveal more than ~2–5% critical errors, lower the pass line and expand rule checks.
4. **Use 2-vote consensus + gold items + MQM-structured feedback.** Require ≥2 independent approvals and zero unresolved critical flags per row; seed gold/control items to measure reviewer accuracy; adjudicate disagreements with a language coordinator.
5. **Gate publication per row AND per language.** Open a language only when a random MQM/ISO 5060 sample passes your quality score and reviewer coverage minimum; English-fallback everything else with a clear "machine translated" label. **Benchmark:** if a language can't reach reviewer coverage or its sample fails, keep it in fallback or buy targeted ISO 18587 review.
6. **Store full provenance and diff by ID+source hash** so each annual cycle only touches the delta and approved rows are reused. Feed accepted edits back into TM/glossary/prompt.
7. **Handle EU/legal items now:** pick CC0 or CC BY-SA for contributions; write a GDPR-compliant volunteer privacy notice with a lawful basis, minimization, retention, and erasure handling; route legal/medical/safety content to professionals, not volunteers.

### Brief alternatives (fallbacks)
- **Agency MT post-editing (ISO 18587):** highest, certifiable quality; use for legal/medical/safety fields and for languages with no volunteers. Cost scales with volume.
- **In-house TMS review:** professional reviewers in Weblate/Crowdin/Phrase without the community layer — simpler governance, less scale, higher cost per language.
- **Scripted pipeline (no human gate):** LLM + rule checks + QE auto-publish. Only defensible for low-stakes, clearly-labeled machine output; rejected here because QE misses hallucinations and numeric/format errors.

## Caveats
- **QE numbers are model- and domain-specific.** Published thresholds (TAUS's 0.88/0.9 bands, cApStAn's project-specific 0.81 cutoff, Custom.MT's 75–95) come largely from vendor blogs and single projects; they are starting points, not universal truths. Segment-level QE is noisy — bucket, don't over-trust — and noisier for distant language pairs (COMETKiwi zh-en MQM Spearman was only 0.268 at WMT22).
- **The biggest operational risk is coverage, not tooling.** Aya and Masakhane both show volunteers cluster in a few languages with a long tail of one-off contributors; expect many languages to need paid review or to stay in English fallback.
- **Anchoring and vandalism are real.** Pre-filled MT biases reviewers; open suggestion queues attract spam. Both are manageable but must be designed against from day one.
- **ISO 5060 and MQM are guidance/frameworks, not pass/fail certifications** — they give you a consistent scoring method, but you still choose the threshold appropriate to the content's use.
- **Some corroborating sources are commercial** (TAUS, Custom.MT, Intento, cApStAn, Crowdin) and some cited papers are recent preprints; treat their specific figures as directional and validate against your own calibration data.
